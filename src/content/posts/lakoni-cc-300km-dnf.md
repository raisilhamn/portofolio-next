## Starting From the Back

I was late — not by five minutes, but late enough to start from the very back of the pack. If you've done a long-distance audax or brevet, you know how bad that position is. The main group was already gone, and I was behind it, riding alone and trying to close the gap.

The problem: the lead group was holding 35 km/h. Not 30, not 32 — 35, on flat ground, with no one to draft off.

I tried. I pushed as hard as I could. But the gap kept growing instead of closing.

One hour into the event, and I was already dropped.

## Clogop Climb: Only 4 KM, Felt Like Twice That

Once I'd accepted I wasn't catching the lead group, I hit the first real climb: **Clogop**. Strava rates it a Cat 3 KOM — just four kilometers.

Don't let that fool you.

Average gradient is 9%, with sections hitting 17%. Not a climb you casually spin up while admiring the view — it's the kind that makes you question why you signed up.

I got through it. Slow, in the easiest gear, breathing controlled. Reached the top with my legs still mostly intact.

## Dlingo to Sermo: Deceptive Rolling Terrain

From Clogop the route continued to Dlingo — rolling hills that look easy but quietly drain you. No single long climb worth remembering, just a steady accumulation of short rises that made my legs heavier without me noticing.

Eventually I reached **Sermo Reservoir**.

That's where everything started to fall apart.

## Cramping, Everywhere

Not the usual cramp. Not just stiff calves or thighs.

My entire body.

Toes? Cramping. Abs? Cramping. Lower back? Cramping. I sat down on the roadside near the reservoir, and every time I tried to move — cramp. Reach for my water bottle — hand cramps. Try to stand — legs cramp. Turn my head — neck cramps.

It wasn't a hydration problem; I'd been drinking enough. It was that 300 km with 3000 meters of elevation gain was more than my body was ready to handle that day.

Maybe not enough electrolytes. Maybe not enough food. Probably both.

## Dusk, 60 km/h, and a Jacket Made of Tissue Paper

There's a long, fairly steep descent after Sermo. I hit 60 km/h at a few points.

By then it was dusk, and the air had turned cold. All I had on was a thin vest — not a winter jacket, not a proper windbreaker, just a vest. My bare arms took the brunt of the night air, and it got colder by the minute.

Partway down the descent, I noticed my breathing had changed. Short. Labored. My chest tightened.

My asthma was flaring up.

I'd had asthma issues before, mostly under control, but the combination of cold air, high speed, and an already-exhausted body was exactly the trigger it needed.

## DNF

At that point I had two options: keep going and risk the asthma getting worse on a dark road, or stop.

I stopped.

It wasn't an easy call — there's shame in it, a feeling of having failed, of not having tried hard enough. But sometimes stopping is the more sensible decision.

I wasn't the only one. **Eleven other riders** also DNF'd that day. Eleven is a significant number for an event like this — proof the route was genuinely brutal, not just for me.

---

*I'll be back. Maybe with a warmer jacket, a better nutrition strategy, and a start time I actually make.*

*Or maybe not. Sometimes knowing when to call it quits is enough.*
